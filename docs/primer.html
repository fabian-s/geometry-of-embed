Representation-comparison primer

A visual and statistical vocabulary for Meetings 4–5

The object being compared

For the same \(n\) inputs, two models produce representation matrices \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) and \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times q}\). Rows are inputs; columns are coordinates or features. The spaces may have different dimensions, so comparing columns directly is often meaningless. Instead, we ask whether the two spaces induce similar relationships among the same rows.

Three notions of similarity

Representational similarity analysis (RSA). Within each space, compute all pairwise similarities or distances between inputs. This gives two \(n\times n\) representational similarity/dissimilarity matrices. RSA compares their entries. It asks whether pairs that are relatively similar in one space are also similar in the other.

Centered kernel alignment (CKA). Convert each representation into a centered Gram matrix and measure their normalized alignment. Linear CKA is invariant to orthogonal changes of basis and isotropic rescaling, but not to every invertible linear transformation. It is a global summary: all pairs contribute.

\(k\)-nearest-neighbour overlap. For every input, find its \(k\) nearest neighbours in each space and measure the overlap of the two neighbour sets. It asks who is near whom, without requiring agreement on all pairwise distances. The PRH literature calls its averaged version mutual kNN alignment (mKNN), but that label should not be confused with the within-space relation in which two points are each other’s nearest neighbours. The answer depends on \(k\).

Global and local agreement are different claims

Two maps can preserve the same local neighbourhoods while stretching distant regions differently. They then have high neighbourhood agreement but need not share a global geometry. Conversely, a moderate global score does not show that the same individual neighbours are recovered. A concrete contrast: two spaces can agree on nearly every input’s ten nearest neighbours while placing the distant clusters very differently — high kNN overlap, mediocre CKA. And one dominant shared principal direction can push CKA high while the individual neighbour sets barely overlap. Neither score substitutes for the other; always name the structure a metric actually tests.

A score needs a reference point

A raw similarity score is not self-interpreting. To build a permutation null, keep the rows of \(X\) fixed and randomly permute the row correspondence in \(Y\). Recompute the score many times. This approximates the scores expected when the spaces retain their marginal structure but the inputs no longer correspond. The observed score can then be compared with this null distribution.

If an analysis searches over many layer pairs and reports only the maximum, the same search must be repeated within every permutation. Otherwise deeper models receive more chances to produce a large score. Width can also affect the null baseline of global spectral measures. Calibration addresses score interpretation; it does not by itself establish a causal reason for alignment.

Questions to ask of every alignment result

  1. What are the rows, and do they correspond across spaces?
  2. Which representation level or layer is compared?
  3. What transformations leave the metric unchanged?
  4. Is the claim global, local, or merely functional?
  5. What score arises under a meaningful null?
  6. Were layers, metrics, neighbourhood sizes, or model families selected after looking at the results?
  7. Does the evidence justify convergence, or only above-chance agreement?
