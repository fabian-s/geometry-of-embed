Session Zero: required preparation before Meeting 1

Session Zero is a mandatory asynchronous on-ramp; please complete it before the first regular session. We want to make expectations transparent and prevent later meetings from becoming remedial lectures.

What you should know before starting Session Zero

  • Representation geometry: vectors, matrices, norms, dot products, cosine similarity, orthogonality, matrix multiplication, projections, PCA at an intuitive level, and nearest neighbours.
  • Statistics for empirical ML: distributions and sampling variation, standardisation, correlation, train/test evaluation, and the distinction between an effect estimate and a visualisation.
  • ML vocabulary: data, parameters, losses, embeddings, activations, and the basic purpose of a neural-network layer.

Critical paper reading skills (i.e. identifying a paper’s comparison population, metric, possible confounds, adjustment, and the conclusion actually warranted by its evidence) will be practiced during the course through the worksheets and previewed in the primer.

Material to refresh or acquire in Session Zero

Use only the resources you need to become ready; nobody is expected to consume every item below from beginning to end.

  • How to read a paper (recommended for everyone): Keshav, S. (2007). How to Read a Paper (two pages): read papers in several passes, not front to back; the second pass deliberately “ignore[s] details such as proofs” and should leave you able to summarise the paper’s main point and its supporting evidence. You do not need to follow every derivation to get a lot out of a paper. Aim to state its claim, the evidence for it, and its limits — which is also what your weekly notes should capture.
  • Reading with an AI chatbot (optional): Pandey, P. (2025). Reading Research Papers in the Age of LLMs combines the three-pass method with prompts and tools for LLM-assisted reading. Chatbots help most with close reading when you use them as a tutor rather than a summariser: ask them to explain one specific passage, equation or figure, to quiz you on a section, or to argue against the paper’s conclusion. Check every answer against the text: they can be confidently wrong and tend to flatten a paper’s caveats. Your notes must still be your own. More generally: Anthropic’s free course AI Fluency for Students (module “AI as a learning partner”).
  • 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra, three chapters (text or video): vectors, linear transformations, and dot products. (Video runtimes roughly 10-15 minutes each.)
  • Jay Alammar, The Illustrated Word2vec and The Illustrated Transformer: read the overview sections, including attention, MLP blocks, and residual connections.
  • Optional reinforcement: StatQuest: PCA step-by-step and, as background only, Distill’s Attention and Augmented Recurrent Neural Networks — soft attention in RNN memory models, not transformer self-attention, which the Illustrated Transformer covers.
  • Course handout: Representation-comparison primer (distributed separately), for terminology needed in Meetings 4–5.
  • Course handout: Concept glossary (distributed separately) — one-paragraph definitions of terms the readings use without explanation; individual meetings point to the relevant entries.

Meeting 1: Word embeddings — what does geometry encode?

Required common readings

  • Goldberg, Y. & Levy, O. (2014). word2vec Explained. Read the complete explainer (five PDF pages; about 4.5 pages of content).
  • Mimno, D. & Thompson, L. (2017). The Strange Geometry of Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (complete; five content pages). Mimno & Thompson use GloVe as the contrast algorithm throughout — read the GloVe and PMI entries in the concept glossary first.
  • Mikolov, T., Yih, W.-t. & Zweig, G. (2013). Linguistic Regularities in Continuous Space Word Representations. Read the sections on the vector-offset method and the experimental results (about two pages) — the source of the analogy-parallelogram picture.

Optional background: Alammar, J. (2019). The Illustrated Word2vec.

Discussion focus: cosine versus Euclidean distance (recall the Session Zero material — neither paper contrasts them explicitly); what vector norms do and do not encode; why the analogy-parallelogram picture is only an approximation.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. What does the skip-gram negative-sampling objective push up, and what goes wrong without the negative samples (Goldberg & Levy)?
  2. What exactly is “strange” about the geometry Mimno & Thompson find, what evidence do they show for it, and which of their manipulations comes closest to showing that negative sampling causes it?
  3. How is the vector-offset method scored (Mikolov et al.), and what simple baseline — more informative than random guessing — would show how much the offset itself contributes?
  4. Mikolov et al. analyse vectors from a recurrent language model, Mimno & Thompson analyse SGNS. Which of this week’s claims are about one specific training objective, and which are claims about word embeddings in general?

Meeting 2: Why should learning create geometric structure?

Required common readings

  • Bengio, Y., Courville, A., & Vincent, P. (2013). Representation Learning: A Review and New Perspectives. Read Sections 3 and 8 only (about five PDF pages in total).
  • Locatello, F. et al. (2019). Challenging Common Assumptions in the Unsupervised Learning of Disentangled Representations: abstract, Sections 1–2, Section 3 through Theorem 1 (the impossibility argument itself), and conclusion (about five pages in total).

Optional background: Olah, C. (2014). Neural Networks, Manifolds, and Topology (complete; about eight print-equivalent pages).

Discussion focus: manifolds, inductive bias, and why disentanglement cannot simply be expected to emerge from data alone — including the impossibility argument, not just its conclusion.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Bengio et al. list general-purpose priors for good representations (Section 3). Which are claims about how the world generates data, and which are preferences built into the learner?
  2. State Locatello et al.’s Theorem 1 in words: why can no method that only sees the data tell apart two different sets of “true” factors?
  3. Bengio et al. (Section 8) cite a sharply decaying Jacobian spectrum as evidence that an autoencoder has found the data manifold. What else could produce that pattern?
  4. Connection to Meeting 1: do the analogy parallelograms show that skip-gram recovered a “gender factor”, or only that some linear structure exists? What would Locatello et al. say?

Meeting 3: The Platonic Representation Hypothesis — claim and evidence

Required common reading

  • Huh, M., Cheung, B., Wang, T., & Isola, P. (2024). Position: The Platonic Representation Hypothesis. Read Sections 1–4 and 6, including Figures 1–9 (Section 5 is skipped; about twelve dense pages). Section 3’s three boxed hypotheses are the paper’s proposed mechanism. Section 4.2 uses contrastive learning and mutual information, both developed properly in later meetings — read it for the claim (similarity \(\approx\) PMI, shared across modalities), not the derivation (see glossary: PMI, InfoNCE).

Optional preview: Platonic Representation Hypothesis project website.

Discussion focus: formulate the claim precisely; distinguish the headline, its empirical evidence, its proposed mechanism, and its acknowledged limits. State what a chance-level alignment score would be and why the paper does not report one. Begin using the evidence worksheet.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. State the hypothesis in one sentence. For each of Figures 2–4 in Section 2, what varies on the x-axis, what is held fixed, and which kind of agreement (vision–vision or vision–language) is measured?
  2. The models in Figure 2 differ in architecture, objective, and training data. What explanation of rising alignment does not need a “shared model of reality”, and does Section 6 acknowledge it?
  3. The mechanism in Section 4 assumes that observations are bijective functions of the world state. Which of the paper’s own data sets violate this assumption?

Meeting 4: Comparing representations — what do RSA and CKA measure?

Required common readings

  • Kornblith, S. et al. (2019). Similarity of Neural Network Representations Revisited. Read Sections 1–3 (four pages). Treat linear CKA (its Equation 4) as given: the kernel-theory derivation in Section 3 is background, not required reading. Sections 1–3 define the method and its invariances; the evidence that CKA works lives in the unassigned Sections 5–6, so this week’s warranted conclusion is “CKA has the right invariances” — no more. CCA, the paper’s foil, is glossed in the concept glossary.
  • Course handout Representation-comparison primer: both “Three notions of similarity” and “A score needs a reference point” (two pages; distributed separately).
  • Kriegeskorte, N. et al. (2008). Representational Similarity Analysis: the introduction and method STEPS 1, 2, 4, and 5 (the dissimilarity-matrix construction and the randomization test); skim STEP 3’s model catalogue and the figure pages (about six pages of text spread over pp. 1–11).

Discussion focus: invariances; global versus local structure (see the primer’s worked contrast); layer selection as a multiple-comparisons problem (previewed here, developed in Meeting 5); and why a similarity score needs an interpretable null baseline.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Why do Kornblith et al. argue that indices invariant to all invertible linear transformations (such as CCA) become useless for wide networks, and which invariances does CKA keep instead?
  2. How is a representational dissimilarity matrix built, and why does Kriegeskorte et al.’s test permute condition labels instead of using a textbook correlation test?
  3. Go through the primer’s “Questions to ask of every alignment result”: which do this week’s excerpts answer, and which remain open?
  4. Connection to Meeting 3: Huh et al. measure alignment with mutual nearest neighbours. What does a high mutual-kNN score not establish that RSA or CKA would?

Meeting 5: Calibration, multiple testing, and what remains of convergence

Required common readings

  • Gröger, F., Wen, S., & Brbić, M. (2026). Revisiting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: An Aristotelian View. Read Sections 3–6 and the main figures (about seven pages). Section 3 defines the null hypothesis and notation that Sections 4–5 use. You are not expected to follow the proofs in Sections 4–5; read them for the stated conclusions and the figures.
  • Koepke et al. (2026). Back into Plato’s Cave: introduction, Sections 4.1–4.3, and discussion (about nine pages in total).

Keep the course primer’s “A score needs a reference point” section available as a reference; it is not additional preparation.

Discussion focus: raw versus calibrated similarity; finite-sample and multiple-comparison effects; global geometry versus local-neighbour agreement. Note that the two required papers disagree about what survives: Gröger et al. find that local mutual-kNN agreement is robust to null calibration; Koepke et al. argue that it may largely reflect the sparsity of the small evaluation gallery — a factor calibration does not test. Come prepared to argue which correction bites.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Gröger et al.: name the two effects that inflate raw similarity scores, and explain in words what the permutation null does about each.
  2. Koepke et al.: what changes between Huh et al.’s set-up and theirs, what happens to mutual-kNN alignment as the gallery grows, and which control shows that the drop is not just a property of the metric?
  3. Koepke et al.’s main text reports raw scores, with the best layer pair chosen on the small subset. Which of Gröger’s effects could this leave uncorrected, and would it matter for a conclusion about a drop with gallery size? (Koepke et al. check this in their Appendix A.2.)
  4. Permutation calibration keeps the sample size fixed; Koepke’s densification changes it. Can a score be far above its null on 1,024 items and still shrink towards zero on a million? Are the two papers then really in conflict?

Meeting 6: Contrastive learning and multimodal alignment

Required common readings

  • Liang, V. W. et al. (2022). Mind the Gap: Understanding the Modality Gap in Multi-modal Contrastive Representation Learning. Read Sections 1–2, 4, and 7 (eight pages in total); skim the intervening theory and application sections for their headings and figures only. This skim is orientation and does not need to appear in the handwritten notes. Section 4’s central result depends on the temperature parameter of the contrastive loss — read the temperature-scaled softmax and InfoNCE glossary entries first.
  • Radford et al. (2021), Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision: Sections 1, 2.1–2.3, Figure 1, and Section 3 through §3.1.2 — the motivation and mechanism of zero-shot transfer, not the results (about five pages). Skip the architecture and engineering details in Sections 2.4–2.5 and the full experiments section.

Assigned secondary branches — prepare exactly one if allocated to a branch group

  • Accessible empirical case: Goh, G. et al. (2021). Multimodal Neurons in Artificial Neural Networks. Long but visual — read as a guided browse, not line by line.
  • Alternative objective: Assran et al. (2023), Self-Supervised Learning From Images With a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture: abstract, Sections 1–3, and the headline results in Section 5 (figures and tables only, so the branch’s evidence sheet has evidence to report; about four pages). Denser than the other branch.

The evidence worksheet is expected for the modality-gap paper and, if branched, for the branch reading. The CLIP excerpt deliberately stops before its evidence sections; for it, “objective described, evidence not assigned” is the honest worksheet entry.

Discussion focus: word2vec, contrastive learning, and CLIP as related objectives — come prepared to connect the skip-gram negative-sampling objective from Meeting 1 to the CLIP loss (no assigned reading makes this bridge; it is a recall-and-connect task); pairwise alignment versus distributional equality; why a modality gap matters for claims of a single shared space.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Liang et al. explain the modality gap in three steps (cone effect, different cones for different initialisations, preservation by the contrastive loss). Which experiment and which model supports each step, and is it about initialisation or about optimisation?
  2. How does CLIP’s pretraining task work, and how does it turn the text encoder into a zero-shot classifier (Radford et al.)?
  3. Liang et al.’s Section 4.4 trains CLIP-style models at small scale with a fixed temperature. How far does that support conclusions about CLIP itself?
  4. Huh et al. (Meeting 3) found CLIP strongly aligned with language models, yet its image and text embeddings sit apart. Which alignment measures from Meetings 3–5 would register a constant offset between modalities, and which would not?
  5. (Branch: Goh et al.) Who judged which neurons are “interpretable”, on what basis, and what do the authors’ own caveats (precision versus recall) imply for the claim that a unit is a Spider-Man neuron?
  6. (Branch: I-JEPA) Besides the loss, what differs between I-JEPA and the methods it is compared with, and does the excerpt isolate the objective as the cause of the gains?

Meeting 7: Inside a model — superposition, features, and residual streams

Required common readings

  • Elhage, N. et al. (2022). Toy Models of Superposition. Read closely: the overview, “Definitions and Motivation,” “Superposition as a Phase Change,” “Superposition in a Privileged Basis,” the dictionary-learning passage in “The Strategic Picture of Superposition” (the “Safety, Interpretability, & ‘Solving’ Superposition” discussion and its Approach 2), and the closing “Discussion.” Read “Demonstrating Superposition” for its figures and headline results only. Skip “The Geometry of Superposition” entirely, along with the computation-in-superposition, learning-dynamics, and adversarial-robustness sections (about 17 print-equivalent pages of assigned text). The figures — phase diagrams, stacked-bar neuron plots — carry much of the argument: view them, do not skim past them.
  • Elhage et al. (2021). A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits: the residual-stream discussion only (about three print-equivalent pages). The overview is optional; terms it name-drops (QK/OV circuits, induction heads, skip-trigrams) are defined in non-assigned sections and may be treated as labels.

Together with Meeting 8, this is one of the two heaviest reading weeks of the course — budget the full three hours.

Optional reinforcement: Nanda, N. (2022). A Walkthrough of Toy Models of Superposition, first 35 minutes. Video only (no transcript) and assumes familiarity with mechanistic interpretability; reinforcement, not a substitute for the reading.

Discussion focus: why one neuron need not equal one concept; feature interference; the residual stream as a communication space; what the toy model does and does not establish about large language models. Each note should also state what the reading does or does not imply about cross-model alignment.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Describe the toy model: what are the synthetic features, what do sparsity and importance mean, and where do the linear and the ReLU model differ? Why does that one difference decide whether superposition occurs?
  2. What changes discontinuously in the “phase change”, as a function of which two quantities, and how were the phase diagrams obtained?
  3. The Discussion checks the toy model against known observations about polysemantic neurons. Could these checks have failed? What would a test look like that could?
  4. The framework paper says the residual stream has no privileged basis. What does that mean, and why can cosine similarity mislead when features are stored in superposition?

Meeting 8: Sparse autoencoders and challenges to clean internal geometry

Required common readings

  • Cunningham, H. et al. (2024). Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models. Read the nine-page main text; skip references and appendices. Its causal experiments report KL divergence — see the glossary entry; no information theory is needed.
  • Engels et al. (2025), Not All Language Model Features Are One-Dimensionally Linear: introduction, Section 3.1, and Sections 4–5 (Section 5.3 may be skimmed; about 6.5 pages in total). Section 3.1 defines irreducible multi-dimensional features (its Definitions 1–3 are needed; the formal tests may be read for their idea only), and Section 4 develops the SAE-clustering method; Section 5’s causal experiments rely on both.
  • Kumar et al. (2025), Questioning Representational Optimism in Deep Learning: introduction, the CPPN/Picbreeder setup at the start of Section 2, Section 4, and the conclusion (about four pages in total). Figures 4–6, which sit in the Section 3 pages, will be examined in class.

Together with Meeting 7, this is one of the two heaviest reading weeks of the course, just before the Christmas break — budget the full three hours.

Optional background (recommended pre-reading for students new to autoencoders or sparsity penalties; see glossary: \(\ell_1\) penalty): Karvonen, A. (2024). An Intuitive Explanation of Sparse Autoencoders for LLM Interpretability.

Discussion focus: interpretable feature dictionaries, nonlinear features, and fractured/entangled representations as challenges to representational optimism. Each note should also state what the reading does or does not imply about cross-model alignment; none of these papers measures alignment directly, so the connection must be inferred (for instance, Kumar’s point that output equivalence is not representational alignment).

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Cunningham et al. offer three kinds of evidence (automated interpretability scores, activation patching on a known task, case studies). For each, what is the comparison or baseline?
  2. The interpretability score uses one language model to describe a feature and another to predict its activations. What could this score reward besides monosemantic features?
  3. Engels et al.: how were the circular features found (Section 4), and which intervention comparison shows that a circle is used by the model rather than merely present (Section 5)?
  4. Kumar et al. compare one evolved and one SGD-trained network with identical output. Why does this show that fractured representations are possible but not how common they are, and what would need to be controlled?
  5. Would any alignment measure from Meetings 4–5 detect the difference between Kumar et al.’s two networks?

Meeting 9: Meaning, grounding, and what geometry cannot settle

Required common readings

  • Bender, E. & Koller, A. (2020). Climbing Towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data (complete; nine content pages).
  • Piantadosi & Hill (2022), Meaning Without Reference in Large Language Models (complete; about five content pages). The paper leans on conceptual role theory, reference vs sense, church-encoding, and embedding theorems without explaining them — read the corresponding glossary entries first, so its strongest evidential moves can be weighed, not just accepted.
  • Harnad (1990), The Symbol Grounding Problem: the introduction and the statement of the symbol-grounding argument, up to and including the Chinese-Room and dictionary-go-round discussion (about three pages). Both of the papers above argue against or from this problem.

No evidence worksheet is expected this week; these are position papers.

Further reading (not expected for this meeting)

  • Michael, J. (2020). To Dissect an Octopus.
  • Harnad (1990), the remainder: the symbolic/nonsymbolic hybrid proposal.

Discussion focus: distinguish the statistical critique of representational convergence from the independent philosophical question of whether structured, aligned representations amount to meaning or understanding. Piantadosi & Hill’s positive evidence — “relational geometry increasingly matches human data” — is a raw-similarity claim: apply the Meeting 5 calibration lens to it. Come prepared to identify one representational claim from Meetings 3–6 that would remain true even if a stronger claim about reference or understanding failed.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Bender & Koller: how do they define form and meaning, and at which step of the octopus story does the octopus fail? Why is the amount of form irrelevant to their argument?
  2. Piantadosi & Hill offer task performance, geometric correspondence with human data, and analogies from other fields. Which of these supports conceptual role specifically, and which only competence of some kind?
  3. Harnad: what is the dictionary-go-round? Does it apply to a neural language model as directly as Bender & Koller assume, given Harnad’s own view that connectionist networks are not symbol systems?
  4. Connection to Meeting 2: Locatello et al. show that data alone do not determine the factors; Bender & Koller argue that form alone does not determine meaning. Are these the same kind of argument?

Meeting 10: Essay practice — debiasing and the alignment hypothesis

Required common readings

  • Gonen, H. & Goldberg, Y. (2019). Lipstick on a Pig: Debiasing Methods Cover up Systematic Gender Biases (complete; about 4.5 content pages).
  • Ramidi, A. & Scharp, K. (2026). The Representational Alignment Hypothesis: Section 3 (seven pages of dense philosophical argument). Section 4 is further reading. Section 3’s empirical premise — that simple linear maps align independently trained embeddings, robustly — is documented in the unassigned Section 2: do not take it on faith; import the alignment evidence, and its Meeting 5 caveats, from Meetings 3–6. The symbol-grounding problem, which §3.2 assumes, was introduced in Meeting 9.

Prepare your handwritten notes as usual: the evidence worksheet for Gonen & Goldberg; for Ramidi & Scharp, which is analysis rather than an empirical study, note which of its five hypotheses (RAH, CAH, AAH, IAH, PRH) the seminar’s evidence actually supports, and on the strength of which results.

Guiding questions for your notes:

  1. Gonen & Goldberg: which definition of bias do the debiasing methods remove, and which experiments show that bias remains? How does their key observation — that most word pairs keep their previous similarity — account for these results?
  2. What is the right reference point for judging the accuracies after debiasing, and is there a circularity in labelling words by their original bias?
  3. Connection to Meetings 4–5: the remaining bias lives in local neighbourhoods, not along a single direction. How does this relate to the distinction between local and global structure?
  4. Ramidi & Scharp: which of their five hypotheses do they defend, which do they reject, and on which do they take no stand? Is their objection to the PRH empirical or philosophical?
  5. Section 3.3 claims that text embeddings can be “precisely” mapped onto visual embeddings. Weigh this against Koepke et al. (Meeting 5) and Liang et al. (Meeting 6).

In class: a dress rehearsal for the final essay session

  • At the start of the meeting you receive four essay prompts, each built on this week’s two papers.
  • You write one short essay (about 35 minutes) on a prompt of your choice, using only your handwritten notes — as in Meeting 11, but with a shorter essay. The practice essay is not marked.
  • The instructors then present two strong and two weak example answers, and we discuss what makes them strong or weak, measured against the marking criteria for the final essays: conceptual accuracy, use of evidence and methodological reasoning, integration across meetings, and clarity of argument (see also the final-essay checklist under Meeting 11).

Discussion focus: the most common ways a short essay answer goes wrong — summarising papers instead of answering the prompt, stating a claim more strongly than its evidence allows, or confusing nearby claims (e.g. “a bias direction was removed” versus “the bias is gone”; “representations align” versus “they capture the structure of reality”) — and how to avoid them.

Meeting 11: Supervised final essay session (three hours)

Six essay prompts will be offered in the session. They are likely to cover:

  1. representation similarity, calibration, and the warranted convergence claim;
  2. geometry and learning objectives from word2vec to multimodal alignment;
  3. superposition, SAEs, and competing accounts of internal structure;
  4. local agreement versus global geometry;
  5. meaning and grounding; and
  6. applications: (e.g. debiasing).

Students answer exactly two prompts using only their handwritten weekly notes for background; printouts of the notes submitted on Moodle are handed out in the session.

Final-essay checklist. An effective answer should:

  • define the claim it is about;
  • distinguish it from nearby claims (e.g. “two representations share local neighbourhoods” versus “they share global geometry”);
  • use specific evidence from the readings;
  • explain at least one methodological limitation; and
  • reach a proportionate conclusion.

A list of paper summaries is not yet an argument.
