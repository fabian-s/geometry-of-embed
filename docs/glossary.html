Concept glossary

One-paragraph definitions of terms the readings use without explanation

The assigned papers occasionally lean on concepts that are neither course prerequisites nor taught in an earlier meeting. This handout gives the one-paragraph version of each (so: dangerous half-knowledge – caveat emptor….) The meetings in which entries are introduced/used are given in brackets.

GloVe [Meeting 1]. A word-embedding algorithm that, unlike word2vec’s predictive objective, fits vector dot products to the logarithms of corpus-wide co-occurrence counts by weighted least squares. In Mimno & Thompson it serves purely as a contrast algorithm: a different training objective operating on the same corpus, so that a geometric property present in skip-gram vectors but absent in GloVe vectors points to the objective rather than the data — a useful contrast, though not a perfectly controlled one (the methods also differ in weighting and hyperparameters).

PMI and implicit matrix factorisation [Meetings 1, 3]. Pointwise mutual information compares how often two words occur together with how often they would co-occur independently by chance: \(\mathrm{PMI}(a,b) = \log \frac{P(a,b)}{P(a)P(b)}\). Positive means “together more than chance.” Levy & Goldberg showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises a (shifted) PMI matrix — the bridge Huh et al. use when they argue that contrastive learners converge to a PMI kernel.

CCA (canonical correlation analysis) [Meeting 4]. Given paired observations in two spaces, CCA finds one direction in each space such that the projections correlate maximally, then further pairs, each uncorrelated with the preceding ones. It is invariant to any invertible linear transformation of either space — which is exactly why Kornblith et al. argue it is too permissive: in the full-rank, high-dimensional regime (width comparable to the number of examples) this invariance can make genuinely different representations indistinguishable. They propose CKA instead.

Temperature-scaled softmax and InfoNCE [Meetings 3, 6]. A softmax turns a vector of similarity scores into a probability distribution; dividing the scores by a temperature \(\tau\) first controls its sharpness (small \(\tau\): nearly all probability on the top score; large \(\tau\): nearly uniform). The InfoNCE (contrastive) loss asks the model to assign high probability to the one matching pair among many mismatched ones under this softmax. CLIP learns \(\tau\) during training, and Liang et al.’s modality-gap result is a statement about how the loss landscape changes with \(\tau\).

KL divergence [Meeting 8]. A number quantifying how much one probability distribution differs from a reference distribution; zero iff they are identical, and it grows as the first distribution puts probability where the reference does not. In Cunningham et al.’s patching experiment it measures how close the edited model’s next-token predictions come to the counterfactual target’s; how many features are patched, and how large the edits are, are separate axes the paper trades off against the achieved KL. Read it as “divergence between two output distributions” (asymmetric, not a true distance); no information theory needed.

\(\ell_1\) penalty, sparsity, and dictionary learning [Meetings 7–8]. Adding the sum of absolute values of a code vector to a loss pushes most entries to exactly zero, so each input is explained by few active components. Dictionary learning uses this to represent data as sparse combinations of learned direction vectors (“dictionary elements”) — possibly more directions than dimensions (“overcomplete”). Sparse autoencoders are dictionary learning implemented with an autoencoder; this is the “Approach 2” the Toy Models paper proposes against superposition.

Logits and logit difference [Meetings 7–8]. A model’s raw output scores before the softmax (which maps the logits (“log-odds”) back to a probability distribution over tokens.). A logit difference (say, between two candidate tokens) is a convenient scalar measure of how strongly an intervention pushed the model toward one output over another.

Conceptual role theory; reference vs sense [Meeting 9]. Philosophy of language distinguishes what a term points to in the world (reference) from how it presents its object and relates to other terms (Frege’s sense). Conceptual role theory is one modern account in that tradition: it holds that meaning is constituted at least partly by a term’s position in a web of inferential and associative relations, rather than being exhausted by reference (reference can remain one optional aspect of a term’s role) — the hinge of Piantadosi & Hill’s argument, since relational structure is exactly what embedding geometry can have.

Church encoding [Meeting 9]. A construction from mathematical logic showing that one formal system can mimic the behaviour of another by encoding its objects and operations internally (classically: numbers and arithmetic inside the lambda calculus). By itself this is an implementation result; Piantadosi & Hill infer from it — and this inference is theirs to defend — that a system can possess genuine structure “about” something it never touches directly.

Embedding theorems (Takens) [Meeting 9]. Results from dynamical systems showing that, under genericity conditions, the relevant state manifold or attractor can be reconstructed — up to a smooth change of coordinates, so distances and angles are not preserved — from a single scalar time series, by using time-delayed copies of it as coordinates. Cited by Piantadosi & Hill as evidence that rich structure can be recovered from apparently impoverished input — the conditions are the debatable part.

Symbol grounding (Harnad) [Meetings 9–10]. The problem of how symbols in a formal system get their meanings without an outside interpreter: definitions only lead to more symbols (the “dictionary-go-round”). Meeting 9 reads the original statement; Ramidi & Scharp’s §3.2 argues — contestably — that cross-modal alignment structure addresses it.

Where you are licensed to skip the mathematics

Three assigned readings contain derivations that are genuinely beyond the course prerequisites. You are expected to read them for the stated conclusions and the figures, not the proofs:

  • Kornblith et al. §3 (Meeting 4): the HSIC/kernel derivation of CKA — treat linear CKA’s formula as given.
  • Gröger et al. §§4–5 (Meeting 5): the concentration-inequality and order-statistics arguments behind the calibration — the propositions’ plain-language statements and Figures 3–6 carry everything the discussion needs.
  • Toy Models of Superposition (Meeting 7): the compressed-sensing and almost-orthogonal-vectors results are quotable as facts; their proofs are outside scope.
